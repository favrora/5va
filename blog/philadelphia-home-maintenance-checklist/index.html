Use one simple record for the whole home

A maintenance checklist is useful only if you return to it. Keep one note or folder with the date, room, observation, photo, and next action. Add product manuals, paint labels, and repair paperwork when you have them. The City of Philadelphia Rowhouse Manual recommends organizing maintenance records and keeping important service information together; that is a practical starting point for any household.

This guide is for noticing and planning small repairs, not replacing a property inspection or servicing instructions. Walk through the spaces you can safely access. Record a loose handle, a door that catches, a new stain, or furniture that still needs anchoring. You do not need to open a panel, climb onto a roof, or dismantle equipment to make a useful list.

Give the list three levels of attention

First separate an immediate problem from ordinary upkeep. Active flooding, smoke, a suspected gas leak, or an unsafe electrical condition needs the appropriate urgent response. Do not leave it on a list for a future handyman visit. Second, flag developing issues such as a recurring damp mark, a fixture that keeps loosening, or several doors changing at once for assessment.

Third, collect the straightforward improvement tasks: a cabinet adjustment, a small dry wall mark, a shelf to install, or a desk to assemble. Give each a desired result and priority. This lets you group compatible jobs without treating every small defect as an emergency. Mark which observations are new so an old cosmetic mark is not confused with a changing condition.

Use spring and summer to review what changed

After wet weather, look from a safe position for a new interior stain or a change near a window or ceiling. Note the date and conditions rather than assuming the source. Around doors and cabinets, record rubbing, loose fittings, and damaged finishes. For a rowhome with a compact entry or rear access, include anything that affects moving materials into the work area.

Warmer months can also be a convenient reminder to review screens, curtain hardware, furniture, and the rooms you use differently. Ask whether a new mounting project conflicts with an opening, radiator, or daily walkway. Leave roof, high exterior, and unfamiliar equipment work for an appropriate service; a ground-level observation and a photo are enough to start the conversation.

Use fall and winter to review comfort and everyday use

When the way you use the home changes, notice doors that do not close comfortably, worn accessible seals, and cold-air concerns around openings. Write down the location and circumstances. A draft does not automatically mean a window needs replacement, and adding a product without understanding the opening can create a new operation problem. Discuss the observation before choosing a fix.

Keep household equipment care tied to its own manual rather than an internet schedule for another model. Check smoke and carbon monoxide alarms according to their instructions and the guidance that applies to your home. Philadelphia’s official fire-safety page provides local smoke-alarm assistance information. Use that source for current arrangements instead of assuming a handyman appointment covers a complete safety inspection.

Make the room-by-room pass concrete

In the kitchen, note cabinet alignment, handles, visible fixture drips, and appliance symptoms. In the bathroom, photograph loose accessories and describe where water appears during normal use. In bedrooms and living spaces, list furniture assembly, mounting, wall damage, and doors. These are observations to discuss, not instructions to reach behind wet appliances or investigate concealed plumbing.

For each job, add the information someone planning the work needs. That may be a product link, dimensions, wall photo, model number, or paint label. If a home has older painted surfaces, mention its age before planning disturbance. EPA provides specific lead-safe renovation guidance for pre-1978 housing. Keep that question early, rather than beginning exploratory sanding to prepare for a visit.

Turn the list into an achievable visit

Choose the top tasks and send them with photos, your Philadelphia ZIP, and access details. Mention rental approval, tenant arrangements, stairs, parking, and the room where each job is located. Ask what can be grouped, which materials to obtain, and what the proposed visit includes. A free conversation and the actual on-site work are separate; discuss fees before booking.

A few useful questions

01How do I choose which small repairs to do first?+

Separate urgent or developing problems from cosmetic tasks. Then choose the everyday jobs that matter most, add photos, and discuss whether related work can be planned together.

02Is this checklist a home inspection?+

No. It helps organize observations and routine repair requests. It does not replace an inspection, emergency response, or the service instructions for household equipment.

03What should I send for a home maintenance visit?+

Send a prioritized room-by-room list, photos, your ZIP code, and any product or paint details. Include access arrangements and who can approve work for a rental property.
